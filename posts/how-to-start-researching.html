How to start researching one topic.

Every research project I have done began the same way: with a subject far too big to study. Mine was sustainable energy. This is how I turned it into one question, one dataset, and eventually a published paper.

November 25, 2026 · Arunkumar Aluru

Starting research can feel like walking into a huge library with no catalogue. There is too much to read, every direction looks important, and it is hard to tell where your own contribution could fit. What helped me was not a clever model. It was a handful of steps I now follow every time I begin something new.

1. Choose a topic you would study without a deadline

Research takes months, and a lot of that time is spent cleaning data, rereading papers, and fixing experiments that do not work. Interest is what keeps you going through that part, so the first filter is simple: would I still want to understand this if nobody were grading it?

For me, that topic was sustainable energy. Solar and wind power are central to the shift toward clean energy, but they have a stubborn problem: they depend on the weather. The sun sets, clouds pass, and the wind drops. Grid operators and planners need to know how much renewable power to expect, and that felt like a problem where machine learning could genuinely help.

It also passed two practical checks. It matters well beyond a classroom, and there is real, open data available to work with. A topic that excites you but has no accessible data is a much harder place to start.

2. Narrow a big interest into one question

"Sustainable energy" is a field, not a research question. The most useful exercise I did early on was to keep narrowing until I reached something I could actually answer:

  1. FieldSustainable energy
  2. AreaRenewable energy generation
  3. FocusSolar and wind, the two sources that change with the weather
  4. ProblemForecasting how much energy they will produce
  5. QuestionHow accurately can machine learning and deep learning models predict solar and wind generation from historical grid data, and which approach works best for each?

A good question tells you what data you need, what you will build, and how you will judge success. If you cannot describe your experiment from the question alone, keep narrowing.

3. Read with a purpose, not just a reading list

Once you have a question, reading becomes much easier, because you are looking for something specific. I found it helpful to start with review or survey papers, which map out a field and point to the important work, before going deeper into individual studies.

For each paper, I read the abstract, figures, and conclusion first, and only read the full method if it looked relevant. Then I wrote one line in a simple table:

  • Data: what dataset, which region, what time period
  • Method: which models or techniques
  • Metric: how they measured success
  • Gap: what they did not try, or where they said results were weak

That last column is the most valuable. After enough rows, patterns appear: methods that keep being compared, datasets that keep being reused, and questions that nobody has quite answered. That is where your own work starts to take shape.

4. Find your data early, and let it shape the question

I learned quickly that a research question has to fit the data you can actually get. For this project, our team used Open Power System Data, an open platform with ten years of hourly German grid data from 2015 to 2024, including installed solar and wind capacity and actual generation. Germany was a strong case to study because it is one of Europe's leaders in renewable adoption, and the dataset was unusually complete.

Even good data needs work. There were missing values to handle, and each one needed a decision that made physical sense. Solar output at night, for example, should not be filled in the same way as a gap in the middle of a sunny afternoon. Correlation analysis and feature importance then showed which variables really carried signal, so we could remove the noise before modelling.

Spend real time with your data before you train anything. Plot it, look at the seasons, find the gaps. Most of my later decisions came from patterns I first noticed in simple charts.

5. Start simple, then earn your complexity

It is tempting to jump straight to the most advanced model you have heard of. Instead, I compared a range of approaches from simple to complex: Ridge and Lasso regression as baselines, then a decision tree, SVM, and random forest, and finally an LSTM, a deep learning model designed for sequences over time.

The baselines were not a formality. They showed how much of the problem simple methods could already solve, which made it possible to see whether the deep learning model was actually adding value. In our results, the LSTM performed best for solar generation, while the classical models remained strong and much cheaper to run. That kind of comparison is far more convincing than a single impressive number.

6. Write while you work

The final step is the one I underestimated most. Writing does not begin when the experiments end. I kept notes on every decision, saved figures as I produced them, and recorded why I tried something, not only what happened.

When it was time to write the paper, most of the material already existed. That work became my bachelor's thesis and was later published at the IEEE International Conference on Intelligent Computing and Next-Generation Communication (ICICNIC 2024). Seeing a question I had narrowed down on paper turn into a published result is a big part of why I am still doing research today.

What I would tell myself at the start

  • Pick a topic you care about, then check that open data exists for it.
  • Narrow until your question describes its own experiment.
  • Read for gaps, and keep a one-line summary of every paper.
  • Look at your data before you model it.
  • Build simple baselines first; make complex models prove themselves.
  • Write notes from day one. Your future paper will thank you.

Research does not start with knowing the answer. It starts with choosing a question small enough to answer well. For me, that question came from sustainable energy, and it shaped everything I have built since.
