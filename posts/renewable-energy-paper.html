Renewable Energy Consumption on Solar and Wind Energy Prediction Using Deep Learning and Generative AI.

A summary of my first published paper, written with four co-authors: what we set out to answer, how we approached it, and what we found.

December 17, 2024 · Arunkumar Aluru

Read the paper on IEEE Xplore ↗

Solar and wind power generate electricity without greenhouse gases or other pollutants, which makes them central to moving away from fossil fuels. Their weakness is that they depend on the weather. To plan for a grid that relies on them, you need to know how much energy they are likely to produce. This paper, my first published research, set out to forecast exactly that.

The question

Can we forecast solar and wind energy production accurately by first finding the inputs that matter most, and can we explain how the model reaches its predictions, rather than treating it as a black box?

The data

We used time-series data from Open Power System Data, an open platform covering power systems across 37 European countries. The data was split into 80% for training and 20% for testing, and the full dataset was then used for validation.

How we approached it

The method moves step by step, from understanding the inputs to explaining the predictions:

  1. SelectIdentify the attributes that contribute most to energy production, using ARIMA, Ridge, and Lasso regression
  2. CompareBuild classical machine learning models (decision trees, random forests, and support vector machines) to validate the results
  3. ModelUse an LSTM, a deep learning model designed for sequences over time, to capture patterns in the time series
  4. ExplainApply Explainable AI (the LIME method) to see which inputs drove the LSTM's individual predictions

An important part of the work was improving the model's performance through careful feature selection, rather than relying on a deep learning model alone to do all the work.

Making the predictions explainable

Forecasts that cannot be explained are hard to trust, especially for decisions about energy infrastructure. LIME builds a simple, local approximation of the model around a single prediction, which shows which inputs pushed that prediction up or down. This gave us insight into how the LSTM was making its forecasts for specific instances, not just how accurate it was on average.

What we found

Using the trained models, the forecast for 2025 was:

  • Solar energy: 72,530 MW
  • Wind energy: 45,280 MW

The bigger lesson for me was that choosing the right inputs and being able to explain a forecast matter as much as the model itself.

The team

This paper was written with my co-authors Pandiselvam Pandiyarajan, S B L Akshaya, Anthapu Archana, and Akula Archana.
